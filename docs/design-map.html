Give or Take

A history quiz that runs on over/under bets, built so that losing a question makes you want to go read about it.

Solo first, multiplayer designed in Relaxed pacing, timer optional Wager round, capped at 50% 10 categories now, 100 mapped Installable web app, mobile first Facts in git, database at phase 4

The idea

Pick a period you already care about

The player chooses a historical period — the Civil War, Caesar's Rome, early Christianity, the American Revolution, America in the 1960s — and answers ten easy questions about it. Questions are phrased as bets: Abraham Lincoln was assassinated. Was that before or after 1870?

Over/under does three useful things at once. It has a 50/50 floor, so nobody feels stupid. It teaches while it scores — guessing wrong on 1870 still plants 1865 in your head. And it needs no hand-written wrong answers, which is the difference between a game with 2,000 questions and a game with 200.

The point is not the score. Every answer opens onto the real fact, the story behind it, and a link out. The game is a doorway into an afternoon of reading.

The name says the mechanic and does a second job besides. Give or take is what an American says about an approximate figure — "1865, give or take." It tells a player who believes they are bad at history that roughly right is good enough here, which is the most useful thing a title can do for this audience.

The core mechanic

Store the fact, not the question

This is the decision the whole project rests on. A category file holds facts, not questions. Each fact is a claim and a number.

{
  "id": "cw-lincoln-assassination",
  "kind": "year",
  "claim": "Abraham Lincoln was assassinated",
  "value": 1865,
  "context": "John Wilkes Booth shot Lincoln at Ford's Theatre on
              April 14, 1865. He died the next morning, five days
              after Lee's surrender.",
  "goDeeper": "Booth's original plan was to kidnap Lincoln and ransom
               him for Confederate prisoners. He only switched to
               murder after the Confederacy collapsed.",
  "source": { "title": "Assassination of Abraham Lincoln",
               "url": "https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/..." },
  "status": "draft"   ← nothing reaches players unreviewed
}

The engine derives the questions at run time. One authored fact yields at least five:

  • Over / underLincoln was assassinated. Before or after 1870? — the easy band
  • Over / underBefore or after 1867? — the hard band, same fact
  • True / falseLincoln was assassinated in 1865. True or false?
  • Came firstWhich happened first: Lincoln's assassination, or Lee's surrender at Appomattox?
  • Closest guessSlide to the year. Points scale with how close you land.

Writing one line of data gets you five questions across three difficulty levels. At 100 categories that is the difference between a feasible project and an impossible one.

Difficulty

Easy means a wide margin, not a different fact

A round

Ten questions, about three minutes

Ten keeps percentages readable and fits a coffee break. The band ramps, so the round opens generous and closes tight — that opening run of easy wins is what makes people play a second round.

No penalties is a deliberate stance. The product goal is curiosity. Docking points for a wrong guess teaches people to answer only when safe, which is the opposite of what this game is for. The wager is the single exception, and it is opt-in by size.

Two currencies

Separate what you know from what you chose to learn

If following a source link earned points, players would farm links and the leaderboard would measure clicking. So reward it on a track that cannot win you a match.

Score

What you already knew. Competitive, per category, per round. Resets every round; your best is kept.

Curiosity

What you went and found out. Earned by saving a fact to your Research List and opening its source. Never competitive — it unlocks new categories and badges.

The Research List is the piece that turns a quiz into the thing you actually described: a list of the facts that snagged you, each with its link, waiting for the next time you have twenty minutes.

Winning

The fairness problem, and Home and Away

If players pick categories by interest, raw scores are not comparable — a Civil War buff playing the Civil War beats a stranger to the period every time. That punishes exactly the behaviour the game exists to encourage.

The fix

Each player picks their own period. Both players answer both sets. Points scored on your opponent's period count ×1.5. To win you have to be halfway decent on somebody else's obsession — which is how the game introduces you to periods you would never have picked.

Who wins, by mode
ModeWinnerNeeds a server
SoloYour own best for that category. Mastered at 90%+ on hard.no

Sudden death

On an exact tie, both players take alternating hard over/under questions from a neutral category neither of them picked, so nobody has home turf. First player to answer correctly on a question the other misses takes the match. Both right or both wrong moves to the next pair. After five pairs it switches to a single closest guess — nearest number wins, and that effectively cannot tie. The optional timer is forced on here.

Accuracy

One wrong date and the game is a liar

A history game trades on being right. Getting Lincoln's death wrong once costs more trust than a hundred good questions earn. So the content pipeline borrows the discipline already running the recipe site next door, and does not get relaxed later.

Rules
Every fact ships status: draftOnly human review promotes it. Nothing auto-publishes.
Every fact carries a sourceTitle and URL, on the reveal card, one tap away.
Contested numbers say soapprox: true plus a note. Civil War dead is 620,000 or 750,000 depending on the historian, and the card should admit it.
Players can report a questionFlagged facts drop out of rotation until someone checks.
Facts live in gitOne JSON file per category. Pull request review is the review queue until the volume outgrows it.

Build order

What gets made, in what order

Each phase is gated on something being true, not on the previous phase merely being finished. The gates are there to stop the project from building a leaderboard before it knows the questions are fun.

  1. 01

    Engine and ten categories

    Four question formats, three bands, the wager, the reveal card, the Research List. Ten categories at roughly twenty facts each — about two hundred sourced facts. Solo only, no accounts, no server, opens from a single file.

    Gate: you play five rounds and want a sixth.

  2. 02

    Party mode on one device

    Two to six players, same questions, pass the phone or gather round the laptop. Cheapest possible multiplayer and the one that serves "all ages" best.

    Gate: a real family actually finishes a game.

  3. 03

    Categories 11 to 100

    Roughly 1,800 more facts. Drafted in bulk, reviewed in batches, promoted only when checked. This phase is the actual product and it is measured in months, not evenings.

    Gate: a repeatable draft-and-review loop that does not depend on heroics.

  4. 04

    Accounts and the Daily Challenge

    First server-side work. One shared round per day, national leaderboard, streaks. This is the retention engine and it is also the first thing that costs money to run.

    Gate: enough reviewed facts to build a fresh mixed round every single day.

  5. 05

    Home and Away head to head

    Matchmaking, the away bonus, sudden death. The competitive game, once there is a population to compete against.

    Gate: daily players who come back without being reminded.

  6. 06

    Toward a thousand

    At this scale categories need parents, tags and search, and the JSON files need to become a database. Deferred deliberately: a thousand categories is a content operation, and it should be built only once the first hundred have proven people want it.

Where the facts live

Git is the source of truth, permanently

One JSON file per category in the repository, through the first couple of thousand facts. Pull request review is the review queue, git history is the audit trail, and it costs nothing to run. Two thousand facts is about 1.5 MB — genuinely nothing.

A build step splits that into a small index — titles, blurbs and counts, roughly 15 KB, loaded up front — plus per-category chunks fetched on demand at about 15 KB each. Fast on cellular, and it is what makes offline play possible at all.

A database arrives at phase 4 alongside the Daily Challenge, not before. That is the first point where something is needed that git genuinely cannot do: per-player history so nobody meets the same question twice, flagged-question reports, the shared daily round, leaderboards.

The rule that survives the migration

Even after the database exists, git stays the source of truth for the fact text, and the database is a serving layer synced from it — the same shape as the recipe pipeline next door. It means a correction to a date still goes through review, instead of somebody hand-editing production at midnight.

One consequence to build for now rather than discover later: in solo play the answers ship to the browser, which is fine. The moment there is a leaderboard, client-side answers are trivially cheatable, so the daily round has to be scored server-side.

Platform

A one-thumb game that works on the subway

Designed mobile-first at 390×844, not as a desktop page that shrinks. It ships as an installable web app rather than an App Store listing: Add to Home Screen gives a full-screen, icon-launched game with no review process, no thirty per cent cut and same-day releases. A service worker caches the category files, so a round still works on a plane. Native can come later, if it earns it.

Still open

Decisions this map does not make

  • Tone policy for the hard periods

    The map includes the Holocaust, slavery, and the Plains Wars, and the audience is "all ages." Over/under betting language on atrocity death tolls would be indefensible. These categories need an editorial rule — likely no wagering, no closest-guess on casualties, and questions pointed at people, resistance and consequence rather than counts. Worth settling before those files get written, not after.

  • Licence, and whether the repository goes public

    The code and the fact data are separable questions. Two thousand reviewed, sourced historical facts have value independent of the game, and giving them away is a decision worth making deliberately rather than by default.

  • Sourcing standard

    Wave one cites Wikipedia, which is checkable and honest but not authoritative. Review should upgrade the load-bearing facts to National Park Service, Library of Congress, NASA and academic sources.

  • Who reviews two thousand facts

    This is the real bottleneck in the whole plan, and no amount of engine work makes it go away.
