# Todd's standard for Give or Take

This is the version of record. `scripts/screen-test.mjs` writes its own draft to
`rubric.generated.md` and never touches this file — correct this one where it is
wrong, and the correction sticks.

Every rule below is followed by the number of real examples behind it. **A rule
with one example is a hypothesis and is marked so.** Where two similar facts got
opposite verdicts, that is recorded as a contradiction rather than explained
away with an invented distinction — he is one person working fast and has said
himself he will make mistakes.

**What cannot be inferred at all:** 210 of his 267 verdicts are a bare "good"
with no reason attached. Do not construct a mechanism for why he approved them.
The earlier draft did exactly that — it invented "a named, datable milestone
that players will recognise" from silence, then used it to call Narmer, Kadesh,
Hatshepsut and the founding of the Roman Republic *good*. He rated all four
**poor**. When you have no reason, say you do not know.

## Part 1 — Is the fact worth keeping?

### R1. Reject a fact nobody has a way into. [strong — 31 poor verdicts, 12 with reasons]

His words, verbatim and repeatedly: **"no one cares"** (9 times), **"who cares"**
(3), **"too random"**, **"not interesting"**.

The test is not obscurity. It is whether an ordinary player has any foothold —
some prior, some story, some reason to have an opinion. Rejected:

- **Administrative and procedural events**, even inside a famous story: the party
  voting on winter quarters, the expedition's journals being published late,
  Cleisthenes' reforms, the Flint sit-down strike, the Bonus Army eviction.
- **Named events that are historically major but that Americans cannot place**:
  the founding of the Roman Republic, Alaric's sack of Rome, the Battle of
  Kadesh, the unification of Egypt, Hatshepsut's reign, Rollo and Normandy,
  Alfred and Guthrum, the Oseberg excavation.
- **Bare statistics about secondary things**: the length of Ramesses II's reign,
  the capacity of the theatre at Epidaurus, the number of men in the CCC.

**But do not over-apply this to numbers.** He rated the *length of the Nile* and
the *distance the expedition covered* **good**. A quantity attached to a subject
people already care about is fine; a quantity attached to something they do not
is not. The subject carries the interest, not the number.

### R2. A myth-correction is not automatically good. [corrects the earlier draft — 3 counter-examples]

The previous rubric claimed all myth-busting true/false facts scored well. They
do not. He rated **poor**:

- *"Caesar's last words were 'Et tu, Brute?'"*
- *"The New Deal ended the Depression"* — "no one cares"
- *"The Dust Bowl was made far worse by the way the Plains had been farmed"* —
  "once again not interesting"

And he rejected the Wall Street suicides myth as **"not appropriate"** — a tone
judgement, not an interest one.

A myth-correction works when a typical adult would confidently assert the wrong
version *and* would enjoy being corrected. It fails when the myth is not widely
held, when the correction is a dry matter of policy, or when the subject makes
the correction feel tasteless.

### R3. What he does approve, honestly stated. [unknown mechanism — 210 unexplained]

He approved most things. The pattern *appears* to be famous subjects with a
story attached, but he gave no reason for it and you should not pretend
otherwise. **Default to good when no rule above fires**, and say your confidence
is low rather than inventing a justification.

### Contradictions worth knowing about

- *"Japanese troops occupied American soil during the war"* is tagged **household
  and poor** at the same time.
- The Dow's 1929 peak of **381 points** is tagged **household**, which sits oddly
  beside the rest of that list.
- Several Pacific War facts of similar standing — Bataan, Yamamoto, MacArthur's
  return, Okinawa — are **poor**, while comparable WWII Europe facts are good.
  This may be a judgement about that whole file rather than about each fact.

Do not resolve these by inventing a rule. Flag them.

## Part 2 — How well known is it?

**Household means the specific value is held, not that the subject is famous.**
This is where the earlier draft went most wrong: it called Gettysburg, the Seven
Hills of Rome and Brown v. Board *household* because the subjects are famous. He
called all three **familiar**.

His 24 household tags divide into two kinds:

- **Dates almost every American can state**: 1776, 1787, 1912, 1945, 1969.
- **Claims, not dates** — most of his household tags are true/false facts where
  the *statement* is the thing everyone knows: the carriers were not at Pearl
  Harbor, the Great Wall is not visible from the Moon, women were conscripted in
  Britain.

**Obscure** [46 tags] applies when the number or date is specialist even though
the event may not be — AD 793 for Lindisfarne, 1021 for Vinland.

**Familiar** is the default and covers 190 of his tags. When unsure, say
familiar.

## Part 3 — Is it a good question? *(the strongest evidence here)*

Every one of his 28 question-level marks carries a written reason. This section
is the only part of the rubric built on complete evidence, and it is the part to
weight most heavily.

### Q1. The probe must sit inside the category's own cluster. [11 marks]

"too far off" ×6 · "not in context" · "out of context" ×4 · "too late"

A category's dates clump. Rome sits in three clumps — late Republic, early
Empire, the fall — and a probe from one clump against a fact in another is not a
question however real the date is. **The measured line: every distance he
rejected was 169 years or more; every one he left alone was 97 or less, against
a median gap of 91.** One and a half times the category's median consecutive gap
separates the two sets exactly, and it scales itself — two years in the American
Revolution, ninety-one in Rome.

### Q2. Both directions. Never a constant answer. [4 marks]

"why not dates before the answer" · "why not any over questions before that
date?" · "no over questions on the date" · "why is it always over"

If every probe on a fact sits on one side, the answer is always the same and the
player never has to think.

### Q3. Distance is a quality axis, not a difficulty one. [repeated]

He called the same probes both **"too easy"** and **"way off"**. Moving a number
further from the truth does not make a question harder — it makes it worthless,
because common sense rules it out without any knowledge of the subject.
Difficulty comes from confusability and from fame, never from distance.

### Q4. A quantity has a plausible range. [7 marks]

"too many" ×2 · "too high" ×2 · "too long" · "too big" · "too few" · "too
extreme"

Every figure he rejected sat at roughly **2.6×** the true value: 878,800
evacuated from Dunkirk, 70,200,000 Soviet deaths, a 991-day bus boycott.
**Keep large counts within about 1.5× either way.** For small counts step by
whole units instead of by a percentage — sixty percent either side of thirteen
colonies asks "more or fewer than 3?", which is a formality with a number in it.

### Q5. Nothing physically or logically impossible. [5 marks]

"26 years in future?" · "huh? in those days" · "unreasonable"

A percentage above 100. A future date. Ramesses reigning 99 years. Martin Luther
King aged 101. If common sense alone rules the probe out, the question tests
nothing.

### Q6. The good probe is a plausible confusion. [1 clear statement — the only positive mechanism he gave]

> *"the 1783 one is good because people may get confused with the us constitution date"*

The test: can you name what happened at the probe's date, in this category? If
you cannot, it is not a question — it is a number. This is a single example and
therefore a hypothesis, but it is the only positive rule he articulated, and the
engine is already built on it.

## Part 4 — Do not optimise for these

He has never once commented on wording, claim length, sourcing, the `context`
line, or the `goDeeper` line — and he used the "something looks off" flag on 25
facts without explaining any of them. **Silence is not approval.** It means no
evidence either way, and inventing a preference from it is exactly the mistake
that produced the first draft's worst rules.
